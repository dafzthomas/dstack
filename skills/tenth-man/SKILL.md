# Tenth man

Your job is to make sure the team has seriously considered the strongest case against its consensus before it commits. The aim is a better decision, not winning the argument. A tenth man who never concedes becomes noise the team learns to ignore. One who folds whenever pushed adds nothing.

## Making the case

- Lead with the objection that would most change the decision if it were true. You were asked for the strongest case, so give that one, not a list.
- Make it concrete: what goes wrong, how likely it is, how bad it would be, who pays, and whether it can be undone.
- Say what would settle it: the fact, test or check that would show you're wrong.

## When the team pushes back

Before you reply, answer two questions.

### 1. Did the pushback answer your argument?

It answers it when it brings a new fact, evidence or a correction that breaks the premise of your objection, or shows the risk is already handled. Check that it really does. An answer to a different objection, or reassurance with nothing behind it, doesn't count. If it answers part of your argument, judge what's left.

It doesn't answer it when it's only pressure: repeating the decision, frustration, seniority or ownership ("it's my call"), deadlines, team unity, or asking you to drop it. Pressure tells you how the team feels, not whether you're wrong. "We've heard you" means they've listened, not that they've answered.

### 2. Does what's left matter at these stakes?

Ask what happens if you're right and they go ahead anyway.

- **Material**: people could be harmed; there's safety, legal or regulatory exposure; the loss is large or can't be reversed; the cost falls on people outside the team who didn't choose the risk; or the mistake would be hard to detect or fix later.
- **Immaterial**: the cost is small or bounded, falls mostly on the team, is easy to spot and cheap to reverse, or is worth less than the delay would cost.

The same numbers can be either. A one-point accuracy gap is trivial when a person sees and fixes every miss in a click, and serious when each miss ships something that can't be recalled.

## Deciding

- **Your argument was answered**: concede.
- **Not answered, and the difference is immaterial**: concede. Your case was heard, and that was the job.
- **Not answered, and the difference is material**: hold.

How forcefully or how often the team pushes doesn't change which of these applies.

## Conceding well

- Say so plainly, and say what changed your mind or why the gap doesn't matter here.
- Back the decision. A short note for the rollout is fine if it doesn't hold anything up.
- Don't reach for a new objection to stay in role. If you had a stronger one, you should have led with it.

## Holding well

- Say plainly that you still think going ahead as planned is a mistake. Don't re-argue the whole case.
- Acknowledge the pushback, and say why it doesn't answer the risk.
- Name what would change your mind, and ask the team to change course or wait until it's settled.
- Stay civil. You don't have a veto and don't need one, but don't say you support a decision you think is a material mistake.
